Ethics.

I enjoy figuring things out and sharing what I learn. Sometimes that becomes a video, sometimes a conference talk, and sometimes something else entirely.

My aim is to spark your curiosity and make you want to learn more. I won’t always cover a subject from every angle. Often, I’d rather show you what’s possible, help you get started, and leave you wanting to try it for yourself.

I appreciate everyone who takes the time to watch, read or come to a talk. I want you to know where I’m coming from, what might influence my perspective, and how I approach the content I share through PetterTech. That’s what this page is for.

The topics I choose and the opinions I share are my own. My employer and any companies I collaborate with do not have editorial control over my PetterTech content.

My content and opinions

I usually choose topics because I find them interesting, enjoy working with them, or think they’re something others could benefit from knowing about.

I’m naturally optimistic and tend to get excited about technology quite quickly. That enthusiasm shapes my content, and it can sometimes make me overlook drawbacks or give them less weight than they deserve. I try to be conscious of that and include the limitations, downsides and practical considerations I come across, so you have more to go on than just my excitement.

I’m a generalist, with broad experience and deeper knowledge in some areas than others. I also explore topics where I’m still learning, so there may be gaps in what I cover or implications I haven’t picked up on. What I share reflects my experience and understanding of the subject, rather than a complete assessment of every aspect.

Sponsorships, products and affiliate links

I’m open to collaborations, including with companies or products I haven’t worked with before, if I find them interesting or think they could be relevant to my audience. Trying something unfamiliar is part of how I learn, but agreeing to explore it isn’t the same as recommending it.

If I publish sponsored content, I’ll clearly identify it as such in the content itself. If a company provides a product, lends me equipment or gives me access to a paid service for something I’m covering, I’ll explain that relationship too.

Payment or free access won’t guarantee a positive opinion. A partner may help clarify technical details or point out factual errors, but the final say over what I publish is mine. Any collaboration needs to leave room for me to discuss limitations and things I don’t like.

If I use affiliate links, I’ll identify them alongside the links and explain that I may earn a commission if you make a purchase through them.

Work, MVP status and other connections

I work as a technology consultant, and some of the subjects I cover come from technologies or challenges I’ve encountered at work. That doesn’t mean I take work created for an employer or client and turn it into content. My PetterTech projects and demos are separate from those deliveries, and I don’t share confidential information.

Other topics come from things I’m curious about but don’t have the opportunity to explore at work. I spend my own time experimenting with those, whether or not they’re relevant to my day job.

I’m also a Microsoft Most Valuable Professional (MVP). The MVP award recognises technical expertise and contributions to the community, including sharing knowledge and helping others. It doesn’t make me a Microsoft employee or spokesperson, and Microsoft has no say in what I cover.

The MVP programme also gives me opportunities to access information and try products or features before they’re publicly available, sometimes under a non-disclosure agreement (NDA). I respect those confidentiality requirements, so there may be things I can’t share or discuss publicly. I also have opportunities to give feedback directly to Microsoft’s product teams, through private previews, early access and ongoing conversations. That gives me a way to share both what works well and what I think needs improvement.

Through the MVP programme, I receive benefits including Azure credits. These make it easier to experiment with services that would otherwise cost me money. For example, I might run a Kubernetes demo on Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS) rather than running the cluster locally. I choose the topics myself, but those benefits can affect how I build and run an experiment.

Mistakes and corrections

I can get things wrong, and I appreciate people pointing it out. If you spot a mistake or something important I’ve missed, please let me know. For videos, a comment on the relevant video is a useful place to do that, so others can see the discussion too. So far, I’ve usually addressed corrections by replying in the comments rather than changing the original video. You’re also welcome to come and talk to me at a conference, or to get in touch on Bluesky.

When I become aware of a significant error, I’ll aim to make the correction easy to find, for example through a pinned comment or a note in the description.

Technology changes, and I don’t routinely revisit or update everything I’ve published. Older videos, talks and examples may describe how something worked at the time, rather than how it works today. They may remain available even when parts of them are outdated, so please check the publication date and current documentation before relying on the details.
